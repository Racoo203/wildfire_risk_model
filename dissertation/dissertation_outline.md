## 1. Introduction & Motivation

- Frame the practical problem: UK wildfire risk is commonly assumed negligible relative to Mediterranean Europe, but Essex incident data (2009–2025) shows a non-trivial and possibly growing outdoor-fire burden that current planning tools don't map spatially.
- State the core research gap: Bouzeraa et al. (2025) built a 14-feature ML susceptibility pipeline for a Mediterranean context; no equivalent spatially-explicit, ML-based susceptibility surface exists for a temperate UK county.
- Introduce the dual purpose of the project: (a) an academic replication-and-extension of Bouzeraa et al., and (b) a deliverable for the Essex Centre for Data Analytics (ECDA), giving it a practitioner audience alongside the academic one.
- Explicitly name the three-part contribution: (1) geographic/climatic transfer of the pipeline to a temperate maritime setting, (2) seasonal stratification as a genuine methodological extension the source paper does not attempt, (3) a Stratified Spatial Block CV with In-Fold Resampling as a leakage-aware CV protocol, benchmarked against standard and plain-spatial CV baselines.
- Justify *why* seasonality matters specifically for Essex as opposed to the Mediterranean case: fire-incident seasonality data (spring/summer/fall carrying ~93% of incidents) suggests fire drivers may differ by season in a way a single annual model cannot capture — set up the EDA finding as the motivating evidence pulled forward from Section 3.
- State the research questions directly: (i) does the Bouzeraa pipeline transfer to Essex with comparable discriminative performance; (ii) does seasonal stratification improve or change susceptibility patterns; (iii) how large is the optimism gap between spatially-naive and spatially-aware validation, and does it change the practical conclusions; (iv) which CV/resampling design choices are responsible for the gap.
- Acknowledge upfront, honestly, that headline performance is currently below the source paper's benchmarks — frame the dissertation's contribution as methodological rigor and diagnostic transparency rather than a performance leaderboard win, and preview that this framing is revisited in the Discussion/Limitations.

## 4. Methodology — Feature Engineering & Labels

- Present the overall pipeline shape (9 orchestrated stages: static → seasonal → labels → integration → preprocessing → EDA → train → evaluate → selection) as a figure, and describe the design principle that each stage is independently cacheable/re-runnable — relevant methodologically because it's what makes the documented ablations (CV strategy, `include_d_fires_as_feature`, label-cleaning strictness) tractable to run as parallel config-driven experiments rather than one-off scripts.
- Explain the diurnal-temperature-range derivation (`tasmax − tasmin`) and the VIF-driven decision to prefer `tasmax + diurnal_range` over the collinear `tas`/`tasmin` pair (VIF > 10 for all three raw variables) — present the actual VIF figures once the EDA stage is finalized.
- Describe the proximity-feature redesign relative to the source paper: splitting a single merged "activity" distance layer into separate `d_buildings` and `landuse_class` layers, and justify this with the near-zero-variance problem the merged layer had (buildings sparse, land-use polygons ubiquitous) — this is a concrete, defensible feature-engineering contribution distinct from the source paper.
- Explain why distance-to-feature rasters (roads/rivers/buildings/fires) are computed via Euclidean distance transform on the *full reference grid before clipping*, rather than on the pre-clipped vector layer, and why the alternative would inflate distances near the Essex boundary — a methodological correctness point worth making explicit.
- Describe the fire-density-to-ordinal-class labelling pipeline: point rasterization → Gaussian-smoothed density (convolution) or KDE → discretization into 4 classes via one of percentile/Jenks/GMM, and justify comparing all three (`compare_classify_methods`) rather than committing to one a priori.
- Explain the GMM-specific mechanics (component-mean sorting into an ordinal remap, threshold extraction via a fine density grid) since GMM is the primary `classify_method` used and its boundaries are less interpretable than percentile cut points — worth a worked example or diagram.
- Describe the frozen-fit discipline for labels: the train-split density classifier is fit once and applied *transform-only* to the test split, never refit — and describe the bug this fixed (an earlier version independently refit GMM on both splits, causing class-boundary desync between train and test that manifested as inflated CV-vs-validation AUC gaps). Frame this as a worked example of why label-generation leakage is as dangerous as feature leakage and easy to miss.
- Present the label-cleaning step (pairwise k-means separating ambiguous "Low" pixels from each higher-risk class) and its sensitivity analysis (n_init/seed/feature-reweight variants, symmetric-difference-vs-baseline metric) as a robustness check on an otherwise heuristic cleaning step.
- Present the Low-only-vs-all-pairs label-cleaning strictness question as a documented ablation rather than a settled choice — describe both variants precisely and state that the dissertation reports results under both rather than picking one silently.
- Present the `d_fires` decision explicitly as a documented leakage-as-experiment-axis: describe exactly how `d_fires` is built (distance to *training-period* fires only, applied identically to test rows) versus the risk that a naively-rebuilt-per-split version would leak future fire locations, and present `include_d_fires_as_feature` as a config toggle whose both settings are reported and compared rather than the leakage variant being silently patched out. This is one of the dissertation's more original methodological framings and deserves a full paragraph on why "document the leak" is preferable to "silently fix the leak" for a replication study.
- Document the three structurally distinct NaN sources and their distinct handling: climate NaNs from sea/estuary pixels outside the HadUK land mask (dropped), slope/aspect NaNs from flat SRTM no-data cells (imputed to zero), and NDVI NaNs from cloud contamination (nearest-neighbour filled) — argue that treating all missingness the same way (e.g., blanket drop or blanket impute) would be methodologically sloppy given the different underlying mechanisms.

## 5. Methodology — Cross-Validation Design (headline contribution)

- Motivate the chapter with the spatial-autocorrelation problem concretely: adjacent 30 m pixels share almost all their feature values, so a standard random K-fold puts near-duplicate rows in train and validation, inflating apparent performance — this is the chapter's thesis statement.
- Present all three CV strategies side by side as the comparison design: Standard (stratified) K-Fold as the naive baseline, plain Spatial GroupKFold on 5 km blocks as an intermediate baseline, and Stratified Spatial Block CV with In-Fold Resampling as the proposed method — a table contrasting what each strategy does/does not control for is a strong visual anchor here.
- Explain the block-assignment mechanism (`assign_spatial_blocks`, configurable `spatial_block_size_m`, default 5 km) and justify the block size choice — ideally with a semivariogram or autocorrelation-range argument if time allows, otherwise as a defensible round-number default with a note that block-size sensitivity is future work.
- Describe the two-pass greedy block-to-fold stratification algorithm in `StratifiedSpatialBlockCV._stratify_blocks_into_folds`: rarest-class-first coverage pass, then general greedy fill — explain *why* a naive single-pass greedy assignment risks starving a fold entirely of the rarest ("Very High") class, and why that failure mode matters specifically for a 4-class ordinal target with a small top class.
- Present the cross-fold class-balance verification the strategy performs automatically (per-class proportion spread across folds, warning above a 0.15 threshold) as evidence the stratification is actually working, not just assumed to work — this is a good place for a small verification table/figure per season.
- Explain the strict isolation principle: resampling is applied only to the training side of each spatial fold, never to the held-out validation fold, and justify why applying SMOTE to a validation fold would be a subtle but serious leak (synthetic points blur the true separation the fold is meant to test).
- Define the "optimism gap" metric precisely (standard-CV AUC minus spatial-CV AUC, or the reverse depending on which was the search objective) and present the concrete found value as a worked example (~0.85 standard vs ~0.73 spatial AUC for summer Random Forest) — frame this as the dissertation's clearest quantitative evidence that spatial leakage materially changes the picture of model quality.
- Describe the "diagnostic, not selective" design: whichever CV strategy is primary (drives hyperparameter selection) still gets scored under the *other* strategy purely for reporting, and this diagnostic pass never feeds back into model choice — this separation of concerns (selection metric vs. reporting metric) is a design point worth making explicit, since conflating them is exactly the leakage failure mode being guarded against.
- Present the Kruskal-Wallis test across models' per-fold spatial-CV AUC scores, per season, as the statistical-significance layer on top of the raw comparison table — explain why a non-parametric test was chosen (no assumption of normally distributed fold scores) and what "significant at 0.05" is being used to justify (i.e., whether model choice is statistically distinguishable or within noise for a given season).
- Discuss the `selection_rule` config as itself a methodological choice worth reporting on both settings: `best_auc` (numerically best validation AUC) versus `most_conservative` (smallest standard-vs-spatial optimism gap among models within 1% of the leader) — tie this back to the source paper's own domain-expert override of its metric-best model in favour of XGBoost, and present your reproduction of that decision logic as a config-driven, auditable analogue rather than an ad hoc override.

## 6. Methodology — Class Imbalance & Resampling

- State the class-imbalance problem concretely with actual counts from a season (e.g., spring: Low 450k / Medium 207k / High 42k / Very High ~5k) to motivate why resampling is needed at all rather than asserting it abstractly.
- Explain the two distinct resampling *contexts* the pipeline separates — hyperparameter-search time versus final-refit time — and justify why they must use different `SMOTEResampler` instances (`is_final` flag): search-time resampling operates on a small subsample per CV fold, so majority-relative targets calibrated for the full dataset would blow the fold size up to a pathological synthetic-heavy set (present the concrete bug: a fold of ~4,800 rows exploding toward ~700,000 synthetic-heavy rows before the fix).
- Describe the two resampling *modes* available (`_resample_majority_relative` — auto or fixed-dict targets relative to the batch's own majority class; `_resample_to_target_size` — fixed total split evenly across classes via combined over/undersampling) and when each is appropriate.
- Explain the explicit-config-wins design principle for `use_smote`/`smote_sampling_strategy` and justify it as a deliberate anti-footgun choice: implicit enabling from a related key (e.g., a target-size being set) is only a *fallback*, and an explicit `False` always wins — tie this to the real incident where `preprocessing` vs `processing` (a key-name typo) silently disabled an intended override, motivating "explicit beats implicit" as a general config-design lesson worth a paragraph in a methods/engineering-rigor chapter.
- Present the resulting after-SMOTE imbalance ratio as a reported diagnostic (with a warning threshold at 1.5) rather than an assumed-successful step, and note this is logged per fold so a reader/auditor can verify the resampling actually achieved its intended balance rather than trusting the config value alone.
- Discuss the trade-off SMOTE introduces specifically for spatial data: synthetic points are interpolated in *feature* space, not real geographic locations, so oversampling can subtly distort the feature-space geometry a spatially-aware model relies on — flag this as a limitation to revisit in the Discussion chapter even though it's not solved within this project's scope.

- Present the block-aware subsampling logic used when the active CV strategy is spatial (guaranteeing every block containing the rarest class is force-kept before randomly filling the remaining row budget) as a refinement specifically necessary once spatial grouping and class rarity interact — a plain random subsample could otherwise accidentally drop the only evidence of "Very High" from the search subsample entirely.
- Describe the Optuna study persistence design (SQLite-backed, resumable across sessions) and the param-space-signature hashing mechanism that forces a new study whenever a model's search space changes shape — explain the failure mode this prevents (`CategoricalDistribution does not support dynamic value space` errors from reusing an incompatible persisted study) as a concrete engineering lesson.
- Explain the `cfg_sig` artifact-scoping mechanism (SHA1 hash of the training-relevant config subset, excluding infra fields like `mlflow_experiment` or `figures_dir`) and why every trained model artifact lives under `models/<season>/<model>/<cfg_sig>/` — this is what makes multiple parallel experiments (different CV strategies, different `include_d_fires_as_feature` settings) coexist on disk without silently overwriting each other, which matters for the ablation-heavy experimental design.
- Present the final-refit protocol precisely: best hyperparameters from the search are refit once on the *full* (not subsampled) training set with final-context resampling, then scored on the strictly held-out validation/test split — reiterate that this is the number reported as "the" model performance, distinct from the CV search score.

## 13. Limitations & Threats to Validity

- State the single-region generalizability limitation plainly: findings are specific to Essex's terrain, land use, and climate, and should not be read as claims about UK-wide wildfire susceptibility without further validation elsewhere.
- Discuss incident-data limitations honestly: operational fire-service logs may undercount unreported small fires, may include false alarms or non-vegetation incidents, and vehicle-deployment counts are a resourcing proxy rather than a fire-severity measure — state how each could bias the density-based labels.
- Discuss the label-construction dependency: susceptibility classes are derived from a density-then-discretize pipeline (convolution/KDE + percentile/Jenks/GMM) rather than an independently validated ground-truth susceptibility scale, so all downstream metrics are relative to a labelling *choice*, not an external gold standard — this is a fundamental, honestly-stated limitation of the whole susceptibility-mapping paradigm, not just this project.
- Discuss the spatial block size (5 km) as a single, round-number choice rather than an empirically derived autocorrelation range, and flag semivariogram-based block-size selection as a concrete methodological improvement for future work.
- Discuss SMOTE's feature-space-only interpolation as a limitation specific to spatial data (Section 6), and note that spatially-aware synthetic sampling (e.g., generating synthetic points constrained to realistic geographic locations) was out of scope but is a natural extension.
- Acknowledge the low-connectivity development window and the 21 August hard deadline as practical constraints that shaped the scope of what could be fully ablated versus documented-but-not-exhaustively-run — this is a legitimate, normal thing to state plainly in a dissertation's limitations section rather than omit.
- Acknowledge that seasonal models are trained on necessarily smaller per-season subsets of an already limited incident record, so per-season models — especially for the rarer "Very High" class — carry more variance than the pooled/annual alternative would; discuss whether the multi-seed robustness check (Section 9) is sufficient evidence this variance is acceptable.
